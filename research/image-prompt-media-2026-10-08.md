# Image prompts: medium-specific visual decisions

- Date: 2026-10-08
- Question: Which medium-specific terms help an image prompt make concrete visual decisions across photography, illustration, painting, comics, graphic design, and physical mixed media? When should each term be used, and which combinations create competing visual instructions?
- Source scope: First-party photography education from Nikon; Adobe learning and product documentation; MoMA, the National Gallery of Art (NGA), and the National Gallery, London; RISO's own technical documentation; and first-party Clip Studio and Toon Boom art-production education. Sources were checked on 2026-10-08. Secondary articles and prompt-engineering lists were excluded.

## Findings from sources

1. **Photographic prompts combine the subject's job with camera and light decisions.** Aperture changes depth of field; focal length changes angle of view and framing; a shallow focus plane can isolate a subject, while deeper focus preserves surrounding information. Nikon's portrait guidance warns that a very wide aperture can leave too little of a face in focus and that lens choice changes facial perspective. Its lighting examples distinguish a broad, soft key, a warm hair light, fill, side light, and backlight. These terms each specify a visible outcome: focus separation, face shape, shadow depth, highlight shape, or background separation. Use them only when that outcome matters; exact f-numbers and millimeters are optional metadata, not a substitute for describing the desired frame. [Adobe: Exposure and focal length](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/photography/discover/exposure-in-photography.html); [Nikon: Aperture and depth of field](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/what-is-aperture); [Nikon: Portrait lens and focus guidance](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/quick-tips-for-taking-better-portraits); [Nikon: Flash photography with a softbox and hair light](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/flash-photography-the-easy-way)

2. **Photography genres call for different evidence in the frame.** A portrait prompt should specify whether the person or their environment is the subject, then choose framing, pose, light direction, and background focus. Product photography should show the object's silhouette, surface, and reflective behavior; Nikon's jewelry example uses a light tent to diffuse and stabilize reflections. Editorial fashion photography uses styling and a concept to tell a story, while e-commerce product imagery prioritizes the item. Documentary photography aims to record real events and ordinary life; photojournalism places images beside reported context and editorial selection. Architectural photography often needs leading lines and controlled perspective: Nikon describes shift as a way to include the top of a building while keeping verticals parallel, and tilt as a way to reorient the plane of focus. These use cases should not inherit one generic “cinematic photo” recipe. [Nikon: Jewelry lighting](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/bright-ideas-tips-and-techniques-for-photographing-jewelry); [Adobe: Fashion and editorial photography](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/photography/type/fashion-photography.html); [Adobe: Editorial fashion photography](https://www.adobe.com/th_en/creativecloud/photography/discover/editorial-fashion-photography.html); [Adobe: Documentary photography](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/photography/discover/documentary-photography.html); [Nikon: Perspective-control lenses](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/the-pc-lens-advantage-what-you-see-is-what-youll-get); [Nikon: Composition and leading lines](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/5-easy-composition-guidelines)

3. **Use lens terms as visual cues, not camera trivia.** Nikon defines focal length by angle of view and magnification; its portrait guidance discusses face distortion and compression, and Adobe describes telephoto framing as making distant backgrounds appear larger. Translate those effects into “wide environmental frame,” “compressed background,” or “natural head-and-shoulders perspective.” If the generator responds to lens numbers, include them after the visible result. Avoid pairing “wide-angle close-up” with “undistorted facial proportions,” or “very shallow depth of field” with “every face and background detail tack-sharp,” unless the contradiction is intentional. [Nikon: Focal length](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/understanding-focal-length); [Adobe: Perspective photography](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/photography/technique/perspective); [Nikon: Portrait lens and focus guidance](https://www.nikonusa.com/learn-and-explore/c/tips-and-techniques/quick-tips-for-taking-better-portraits)

4. **Line art, flat-color illustration, and painterly rendering encode form differently.** Adobe describes line art as monochrome marks whose line weight and cross-hatching can carry light, shadow, and volume. Adobe's cel-shading guide describes block color and shadow with little blending; Clip Studio's anime-coloring guide describes simple, unblended shading as a practical animation convention, while allowing limited gradient or glow effects as accents. Shape-focused prompts should say what geometry changes: silhouette, proportion, corner roundness, contour rhythm, or shape size. Adobe's Illustrator teaching material connects rounded corners with a softer look and stroke weight with emphasis. Avoid assuming universal meanings such as “circles are friendly”; the source material supports controllable geometry, not fixed emotional symbolism. [Adobe: Line art](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/illustration/discover/line-art.html); [Adobe: Cel shading](https://www.adobe.com/uk/creativecloud/animation/discover/cel-shading.html); [Clip Studio: Anime-style coloring](https://www.clipstudio.net/how-to-draw/archives/162911); [Adobe: Refine shapes and stroke weight](https://www.adobe.com/learn/illustrator/paths/illustration/illustrator/series/graphic-design/create-a-fun-digital-illustration/illustrator/in-app/refine-the-details-of-an-illustration)

5. **Edge control and paint application determine how marks meet and how the surface reads.** A prompt can distinguish crisp, hard-edged flat areas from soft transitions or boundaries that merge into adjacent colors; this changes silhouette clarity and whether the image reads as graphic or atmospheric. MoMA describes hard-edge painting through sharply delineated geometry, and Adobe notes that line weight can make light and shadow legible. For physical painting, MoMA defines impasto as thick paint with visible brush or palette-knife marks. NGA explains that wet-on-wet blends new paint into still-wet paint and therefore favors working quickly. NGA defines glazing as transparent layers that influence colors or tonal values below; the National Gallery, London notes that a transparent glaze modifies the color underneath without obscuring it. Use impasto for raised, tactile strokes; wet-on-wet for intermingling colors and softened transitions; glazing for translucent color depth. “Glaze this passage” and “blend this same passage wet into wet” specify different paint behavior; assign them to separate layers or passes if both are wanted. [MoMA: Hard Edges, Expanded Fields](https://www.moma.org/calendar/galleries/5888); [Adobe: Line art and line weight](https://www.adobe.com/creativecloud/illustration/discover/line-art.html); [MoMA: Impasto](https://www.moma.org/momaorg/shared/flattened_pages/moma_learning/glossary/); [NGA: Painting techniques](https://www.nga.gov/educational-resources/explore-basics-painting); [NGA: Technical glossary](https://www.nga.gov/research/publications/technical-glossary); [National Gallery, London: Glaze](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/glossary/glaze)

6. **Comic and anime terms specify production conventions, not just a broad “Japanese style.”** Clip Studio's official manual separates screentone frequency (lines per inch and dot size) from density (how much tone is present); pattern and angle are separate choices too. Its comics feature guide identifies panels, speech balloons, action lines, speed lines, saturated lines, and tones as distinct tools. Use those terms when the desired image is a manga/comic page, panel, or printed screentone treatment. Toon Boom's character turnaround lesson uses guide lines to keep views consistent and treats inking, cleanup, and color-model painting as separate stages; “character turnaround/model sheet” therefore signals repeatable character design across views, not a single dramatic pose. A dense dot tone and smooth airbrushed gradients compete when both are applied to the same tonal region; a comic page can combine them if the prompt assigns tones to shadows/backgrounds and gradients to a deliberate glow or color accent. [Clip Studio: Screentone frequency, density, type, and angle](https://help.clip-studio.com/en-us/manual_en/540_comic/Screentones.htm); [Clip Studio: Comic effects and tone tools](https://www.clipstudio.net/en/comics-manga/tool/effects-tones.html); [Clip Studio: Anime-style cel shading](https://www.clipstudio.net/how-to-draw/archives/162911); [Toon Boom: Inking and colouring a character turnaround](https://learn.toonboom.com/modules/character-model-clean-up-and-colour1/topic/activity-1-inking-and-colouring-a-character-turn-around1)

7. **Graphic-design terms control hierarchy and print behavior.** In Adobe's layout guidance, type size, weight, and color establish hierarchy; kerning adjusts a particular letter pair, tracking spaces a selected range, and leading controls the distance between text baselines. A grid organizes alignment and relationships; negative space separates groups and makes the focal information easier to see. Halftone means dot-based tonal simulation; frequency changes dot scale while density changes dot coverage. Duotone maps grayscale information to two custom inks, so it limits palette and changes tonal color rather than simply adding a two-color overlay. RISO's own documentation describes a master wrapped around a drum, with ink passing through its perforations onto paper; its digital duplicator models can print with separate color drums. Therefore “risograph” implies a stencil-print process and spot-color layers. Specify the number or colors of inks, dot scale, paper, and whether visible registration offsets are desired. RISO's sources establish the process and multiple drums, but do not say that misregistration is mandatory; treat offsets as an intentional visual artifact. Do not ask the same area to be both a clean continuous-tone photograph and a coarse monochrome halftone; use selective halftone or assign the two treatments to different layers. [Adobe: Layout hierarchy, grid, and negative space](https://www.adobe.com/learn/illustrator/web/layout-basics); [Adobe: Kerning and tracking](https://helpx.adobe.com/indesign/desktop/format-and-style-text/tabs-indents-and-spacing/about-kerning-and-tracking.html); [Adobe: Leading](https://helpx.adobe.com/indesign/desktop/format-and-style-text/character-formatting/adjust-line-spacing-with-leading.html); [Clip Studio: Screentone frequency and density](https://help.clip-studio.com/en-us/manual_en/540_comic/Screentones.htm); [Adobe: Duotone image behavior](https://helpx.adobe.com/photoshop/desktop/adjust-color/color-effects-techniques/create-and-edit-duotone-images.html); [RISO: RISOGRAPH ink and master process](https://www.riso.co.jp/english/tech_portal/core/ink.html); [RISO: Two-color digital duplicators](https://www.riso.co.jp/english/product/digital_dup/index.html)

8. **Material cues should name the actual construction and its visible evidence.** MoMA defines collage as fragments of paper or other material arranged and fixed to a support, papier-collé as cut-and-pasted paper, mixed media as two or more media combined, textile as a work made from fibers, and ceramics as clay hardened by heat. Translate those processes into visible cues: torn or cut edges, overlaps, seams, weave, folds, surface relief, fingerprints, tool marks, glaze, or cast shadows. These are practical inferences from material and construction terms, not a claim that every artwork in a medium must show every artifact. “Paper collage with raised cut-paper layers” has a different depth and edge logic from “smooth seamless digital vector.” “Hand-molded clay” suggests sculpted volume and tool marks; “glazed ceramic” adds a fired, reflective surface. For mixed media, name a dominant medium and limit secondary media to specific layers or elements so the surface does not receive contradictory texture instructions. [MoMA: Art terms glossary](https://www.moma.org/momaorg/shared/flattened_pages/moma_learning/glossary/); [MoMA: Textile terms](https://www.moma.org/collection/terms/?filter=T); [MoMA: Materials glossary for collage and print](https://www.moma.org/interactives/objectphoto/materials/glossary.html)

## Inferences for the skill

- Route by **medium first**, then by the medium's own task or subgenre. “Portrait” and “product hero” are photographic purposes; “cel shading” and “screentone” are rendering conventions; “risograph” is a print process; “collage” and “clay” name construction/material. These are different axes, so a single flat list of style adjectives will blur rather than clarify them.
- Give each selected term a visible consequence and use condition. For example: “telephoto compression” → tighter framing and visually larger background layers; use when depth should feel compressed. “Screentone at low frequency” → visibly larger dots; use when print texture should read at a glance. “Impasto” → raised, visible paint marks; use when surface tactility matters.
- Keep contradictory terms only when each is assigned a role or region: hard ink contour with soft watercolor fill; sharp subject edges with lost background edges; risograph dots over a photographic subject; typography on a collage poster. Otherwise prefer one dominant rendering logic and a few compatible secondary cues.
- Treat references as evidence of a visual target, not as permission to copy all their vocabulary. Identify which layer the reference controls (lighting, palette, line, grid, material, or camera) and do not import unrelated terms from other layers.
- The sources support the meaning of the craft terms; they do not measure how reliably a particular image model follows them. Validate the reference instructions against the selected generator and revise wording when output evidence shows a mismatch.

## Limitations

- These sources explain photographic, artistic, editorial, and production techniques. They do not establish a universal taxonomy of image styles, fixed emotional meanings for shape types, or a complete vocabulary for every school, culture, or game-art pipeline.
- Technical values such as focal length, f-number, halftone frequency, and ink count describe real capture or production settings. Image generators may use them as loose style cues and may not render them literally.
- Adobe and Nikon education pages include practitioner examples and suggested starting points, not universal rules. Genre boundaries such as “editorial” also vary by context; fashion editorials and journalistic/photo-editorial images have different goals.

## Supplemental source check: 2026-10-08

The implementation added more specific vocabulary. The following facts were checked against additional primary sources; the suggested prompt use is an inference, not a source requirement.

- Corel's indoor-lighting guide distinguishes split, Rembrandt, and butterfly lighting using visible face-shadow patterns. The skill uses these labels to select a face-lighting result, not to prescribe identical lighting for every portrait. [Corel: Indoor lighting setups](https://learn.corel.com/wp-content/uploads/2022/03/INDOOR-LIGHTING-SETUPS-FOR-PHOTOGRAPHY.pdf)
- The National Gallery describes sfumato as blurred contours and gradual tonal transitions. The skill uses this for edge and tone decisions, rather than as generic softness. [National Gallery: Sfumato](https://www.nationalgallery.org.uk/paintings/glossary/sfumato)
- Winsor & Newton describes granulation as mottled color appearance. The skill distinguishes this material effect from adding uniform digital noise. [Winsor & Newton: Granulation](https://www.winsornewton.com/blogs/guides/what-is-granulation-medium)
- NGA distinguishes woodcut, lithography, and screenprinting through their different image-bearing surfaces and transfer methods. The skill maps them to different line, plane, and color-layer decisions. [NGA: Printmaking basics](https://www.nga.gov/educational-resources/printmaking-basics)
- The National Gallery of Australia describes gouache opacity and oil paint raised by brushes or palette knives. The skill treats coverage and paint relief as separate choices. [National Gallery of Australia: Materials of Art](https://nga.gov.au/exhibitions/materials-of-art/)
- The V&A defines quilling by rolling narrow paper strips into shapes. Its making resources distinguish knitting, crochet, and embroidery. The skill preserves construction-specific cues rather than treating all fiber crafts or paper crafts as one texture. [V&A: Paper quilling conservation](https://www.vam.ac.uk/__data/assets/pdf_file/0010/177598/34803_file.pdf); [V&A: Make and do](https://www.vam.ac.uk/info/make-and-do)

The added references remain decision guides, not a complete art vocabulary or a tested ranking of prompt terms for a particular generator.

- Profoto's portrait case study distinguishes short lighting from broad lighting by the face area closest to the camera. This supplements Corel's guide, which does not define broad/short. [Profoto: Character portraits](https://www.profoto.com/nl/en/still-photography/profoto-stories/character-portraits-with-john-russo-and-profoto-d2)
- The National Gallery of Australia's Picasso print guide distinguishes engraving from etching within intaglio. [National Gallery of Australia: Picasso Vollard Suite](https://nga.gov.au/exhibitions/picasso-vollard-suite-prints/)
